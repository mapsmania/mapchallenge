THE COASTLINE PARADOX

The Never-
Ending Coastline

How long is a coastline? In cartography and mathematics, this seemingly simple question has no single answer—a phenomenon known as the coastline paradox.

Unlike a smooth geometric shape, a natural shoreline is self-similar and irregular at every scale. The smaller the ruler you use to measure it, the longer the coastline becomes. Scroll down to watch this paradox unfold across three resolutions of the same shore.

Richardson’s Discovery

In the 1950s, English mathematician Lewis Fry Richardson noticed that Spain and Portugal reported wildly different lengths for their shared border—simply because each country mapped it at a different scale.

Why It Doesn’t Converge

If you measure a smooth circle with smaller and smaller rulers, your total approaches a fixed limit (2πr). A fractal coastline never settles on a limit—each new level of detail uncovers previously hidden perimeter.

Mandelbrot & Fractal Dimension

In 1967, Benoit Mandelbrot used this exact problem to introduce fractal geometry. Because rugged coastlines have a fractal dimension greater than 1 (around 1.25 for Britain), their measured length obeys a power law that grows without bound as scale shrinks.

SIDE-BY-SIDE COMPARISON

Three rulers, three different lengths.

On the map, all three resolutions are now straightened side by side from the exact same starting point. Even though they represent the same stretch of coast, every increase in cartographic detail adds measurable distance.

In pure mathematics, an idealized fractal coastline has an infinite perimeter enclosing a finite area. In real-world cartography, every published coastline figure is only meaningful when paired with the scale and resolution at which it was measured.

Data note: All three coastline traces are generated from inner.geojson.
